# Portfolio Documentation — Early Career & Formation

> This document covers the professional and personal experiences that shaped who I am. It is intended not as a resume, but as a human record — the story of how a competitive swimmer, engineering student, teacher, and curious problem-solver became someone ready to tackle real-world challenges.

---

## Education

**Degree:** Computer Science Engineering *(Ingeniería Civil Informática)*
**Institution:** Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez (UAI) — Santiago, Chile
**Period:** Approximately 2014 – 2020
**Grade:** 5.8 / 7.0 *(equivalent to approximately 3.4–3.6 GPA on the US 4.0 scale; specialization years consistently above 6.0, equivalent to ~3.7–3.9 GPA)*

---

### How I Got Here

My path into Computer Science wasn't a straight line. I was always strong in math, grew up enjoying problem-solving, and come from a family with an engineering background — my father is an engineer. My first choice of university was Chemistry Engineering, and when I wasn't accepted, I enrolled at UAI for a general Engineering program. During that first year, I was required to choose a specialization — and I fell in love with programming through C++ and PHP. The choice became obvious. I never looked back.

The degree itself is a 5-to-6 year professional program — structurally closer to a combined Bachelor's + pre-graduate curriculum than a standard 4-year CS degree. It included academic coursework, pre-graduate level courses (equivalent in content to early Master's coursework, though not formally titled as such), and a 6-month professional internship.

---

### Academic Journey

The first couple of years were a genuine shock. The jump in academic rigor from Chilean high school to university is steep, and adjusting took time. But once I found my footing — particularly during the specialization years — my performance reflected my actual engagement: consistently above 6.0, which maps to honors-level work by US standards.

---

### UAI's Professional Formation Pillar

UAI is well known in Chile not just for technical quality but for producing professionals, not just technicians. Alongside the engineering curriculum, the university runs a structured set of courses specifically designed to develop the human and professional side of students. I completed:

- **Leadership** (multiple courses)
- **Written Expression**
- **Oral Expression** (multiple courses)
- **Critical Reading**
- **Argumentation and Critical Thinking**

For a Computer Science program, this is unusual — and it turned out to be the most consequential part of my formation.

---

### What I Actually Learned

The most valuable thing I took from UAI wasn't a technical skill. It was learning to communicate, express myself, and lead others. Of all the courses in that formation pillar, **Leadership** changed me the most — but not in the way I expected.

I came in thinking that communicating well was enough. The course gradually dismantled that assumption. What it showed me, across a full semester of friction and reflection, was that real leadership requires something deeper than clarity: it requires understanding what the other person is actually going through. Only when you understand what matters to someone can you connect their personal objectives to a shared goal. That realization landed slowly — it wasn't a single moment, but an accumulation across the semester. And it stayed.

That insight — that communication without empathy is just noise — became a quiet foundation for everything that came after.

## Teaching — School of Tech

**Role:** Instructor / Co-owner (later project)
**Company:** School of Tech *(schooloftech.cl)*
**Location:** Santiago, Chile (and one additional country)
**Period:** January 2017 – February 2021
**Type:** Part-time, alongside university studies (up to 30 hours/week, including summers)

---

### What the Work Was

School of Tech is an educational technology company that brings programming, robotics, and innovation courses to schools and institutions. I joined as an instructor and stayed for four years — long enough to grow into something considerably more than a classroom teacher.

Over that period, my work expanded across three distinct layers:

**1. Teaching kids (ages 10–18)**
I taught programming courses covering video games, electronics, and robotics across 7 educational institutions in 2 countries. The students ranged from 10-year-olds encountering code for the first time to teenagers building their first electronic projects.

**2. Training teachers**
I became an instructor for technology tools — Google Docs, Google Sheets, Google Classroom, and collaboration platforms — for over 750 teachers across various educational establishments. This meant walking into rooms full of professional educators, as a university student, and helping them transform how they work.

**3. Entrepreneurship mentorship**
I mentored 5 teams of Starbucks staff in the development of technological entrepreneurship projects. One of those teams placed **second at the regional level**.

---

### The Co-ownership Chapter

After several years of consistent, reliable work, School of Tech's leadership recognized something in me beyond instructional ability: organization, professionalism, and accountability. They offered me a co-ownership role in a new internal project — a program designed to teach teachers how to transform their practice with technology.

This was the first time I stepped fully outside the classroom and into something closer to business. My responsibilities included:

- Conducting **statistical analysis of teaching competencies** across a dataset of over 2,000 teachers
- **Designing data visualizations** to communicate market research findings
- **Building presentations** (PowerPoint) used directly in sales — showing prospective institutional clients how technology adoption improved outcomes in schools already using our methods
- **Participating in academic design** for teacher training workshops

Looking back, this was product thinking and go-to-market work — I just didn't have that vocabulary yet. I was identifying a market need, quantifying it, and packaging it into a compelling pitch. It was the first time I tasted that kind of work, and I knew immediately it was closer to what I actually wanted to do with my career.

---

### What Four Years of Teaching Taught Me

The range of audiences at School of Tech was extreme — and that range was the education.

Teaching a 10-year-old the foundations of robotics requires a completely different approach than mentoring adults developing entrepreneurship ideas, which is completely different again from standing in front of a room of professional teachers — as a university student — and convincing them to change how they work.

Each of those contexts demanded a different way of thinking, a different register of communication, a different version of myself. Over four years, I learned to code-switch fluently between technical depth and accessible simplicity, between authority and humility, between instruction and facilitation.

This wasn't a skill I learned in a course. It was built through repetition — hundreds of classes, dozens of workshops, thousands of small adjustments. By the time I left, I could walk into almost any room and find the right way to communicate something complex to someone who didn't share my technical background.

## Teaching — Silabuz

**Role:** Mentor / Guest Instructor
**Company:** Silabuz *(no longer in operation as of December 2022)*
**Period:** Approximately 2019–2020 (approximately 10 events total)
**Type:** Freelance / event-based, alongside university studies

---

### What the Work Was

Silabuz brought me in for a small number of specific events — roughly 10 in total — as a mentor for high school students working on personal technology projects.

The focus was different from School of Tech. Rather than teaching technical skills directly, my role was to shape how students *think*:

- How to approach a problem as an entrepreneur, not just a student
- How to apply critical thinking when confronting new technologies
- How to move through **innovation circles**: brainstorming, discovery, research, validation

It was brief and targeted, but it reinforced something I was already learning at SOT: that teaching methodology and mindset is often more valuable than teaching content.

## Waiter — Amelia Izquierdo Catering

**Role:** Event Staff / Waiter
**Company:** Amelia Izquierdo *(private catering company, word-of-mouth only, no website)*
**Period:** 2016 – 2019 (0 to 3 nights per week, alongside university and teaching)
**Type:** Part-time, event-based

---

### What the Work Was

Amelia Izquierdo is a private catering company that operates entirely through word-of-mouth referrals. Each shift was an event — a different venue, a different client, a different setup — which meant no two nights were the same and there was no room for a bad performance. You had one shot.

The work covered the full arc of an event: preparation (dessert tables, kitchen assistance, flowers, table setup) through service (welcoming and attending guests). Physical, fast-paced, and entirely people-facing.

I was doing this from 2016 to 2019 — simultaneously studying engineering, teaching kids and teachers at School of Tech, and serving at private events several nights a week. At certain points, all three were running in parallel.

---

### What It Taught Me

This is the experience I least expected to be formative, and it was.

Event catering is unglamorous and invisible work. No one at the table knows or cares that you're an engineering student with leadership coursework and a part-time teaching career. You are there to serve, and that is the whole of it.

What I came away with wasn't a lesson about hustle or stress management — though both were tested. It was something quieter and more lasting: **no one is only what they appear to be.**

People have stories. Everyone at every table, and everyone in every kitchen, is going through something. There are remarkable people everywhere — and everyone deserves the chance to prove themselves, regardless of the role they're currently playing.

That value — a fundamental, unpretentious respect for people — didn't come from a leadership course. It came from showing up to events, doing invisible work, and paying attention.

## Personal Dimension

### The Swimmer

Before university, before teaching, before any of the professional story — there was swimming.

I competed as a swimmer for **9 years**, from childhood through high school. At the peak of training, my daily schedule looked like this:

- **4:00 AM** — Wake up, breakfast
- Bike to the club
- **2 hours** of swimming
- Bike to school
- Full school day
- Bike back to the club
- **1 hour** of gym
- **2 more hours** of swimming
- Bike home
- **Home by 8:30 PM**

This was the routine. Saturdays included. For years.

I trained alongside people who went on to compete at the Olympics.

I don't tell this story to impress — I tell it because it is the most honest explanation of who I am at work. That schedule, maintained across childhood and adolescence, didn't just build fitness. It built a complete operating system: organized, structured, proactive, someone who does not leave for tomorrow what can be done today. Those aren't values I adopted later in life. They were installed early, through repetition and physical consequence.

When I eventually quit competitive swimming before university, I carried that system with me into everything else.

---

### Who I Am

Outside of structured commitments, I invested my university years in building a social life and being present for the people around me. I'm someone who shows up — for friends, for family, for whoever needs something done. Reliability isn't a professional trait for me; it's personal.

The people closest to me would describe me as: a good friend, funny, happy, trustworthy, reliable, honest.

They would also say my biggest blind spot is how demanding I am with myself. I hold my own performance to a standard that can become personal — I get invested at a level that goes beyond the professional. It's not perfectionism for its own sake; it's caring too much. The same quality that makes me reliable makes me hard on myself when things fall short.

I'm aware of it. I don't think it's going away.

---

### What Drives Me

There was no single moment of conversion into tech. The path assembled itself:

- A natural aptitude for math and problem-solving, inherited partly from an engineering household
- A first year of university that introduced programming and made it click
- Four years of teaching that showed me I could communicate complexity to anyone
- A co-ownership role at School of Tech that revealed what I actually wanted to do: analyze, build, solve real problems, grow
- A growing restlessness with pure teaching, and a clear pull toward work that created something tangible

I'm not someone who is satisfied operating inside a fixed lane. I want to understand the full picture — the technical problem, the human need behind it, and the clearest path between the two.
